\section{Introduction}
Lithium-ion batteries are central to electric vehicles, portable electronics, and stationary energy storage because they combine high energy density, low self-discharge, and long cycle life. Their performance nevertheless deteriorates through several coupled processes, including solid-electrolyte-interphase growth, lithium plating, loss of lithium inventory, and loss of active material. These mechanisms appear at the cell level as capacity fade, power fade, and increasing impedance, and their interactions make degradation difficult to characterize from a single measurement \cite{edge2021degradation,birkl2017degradation}. Accurate state-of-health (SOH) estimation is therefore essential for dependable energy management, maintenance scheduling, and safe operation.

Early SOH estimators were largely built on electrochemical or equivalent-circuit models. Electrochemical models provide interpretable descriptions of transport and reaction dynamics, and observer-based methods can infer internal battery states from their governing equations \cite{moura2017battery}. Equivalent-circuit models reduce the computational burden by representing terminal behavior with a compact network of electrical elements. In practice, however, both families depend on model structure and parameter identification. Their accuracy can deteriorate when temperature, load profile, and dominant aging mechanism differ from the calibration conditions, while high-fidelity electrochemical models may be too costly for repeated onboard inference \cite{berecibar2016critical}.

The increasing availability of cycling measurements has shifted attention toward data-driven estimation. Conventional machine-learning methods construct health indicators (HIs) from voltage, current, temperature, time, resistance, or derived electrochemical curves and learn a regression from these indicators to SOH. Such models can achieve strong accuracy with moderate computational cost; for example, machine-learning estimators have jointly inferred battery charge and health states from routinely measured signals \cite{ng2020predicting}. Data-driven analysis has also shown that informative signatures of lifetime can be extracted before pronounced capacity degradation becomes visible \cite{severson2019datadriven}. However, the resulting performance often depends on feature engineering and on whether the selected HIs remain observable under partial charging, altered protocols, or unseen cells.

Deep neural networks reduce the reliance on manually specified mappings by learning representations directly from cycling sequences. Convolutional models efficiently extract local patterns, whereas recurrent and gated architectures describe the evolution of degradation across cycles. Dedicated deep-learning frameworks have demonstrated the value of automatically learned battery features for SOH estimation \cite{fan2020novel}, and hybrid convolutional--recurrent models can combine local feature extraction with bidirectional temporal modeling \cite{mazzi2024hybrid}. These task-specific networks are nevertheless trained with comparatively small labeled aging datasets, which can limit their robustness when cell characteristics or operating conditions change. They also face difficulty in representing capacity regeneration: short-lived upward variations may interrupt the overall fading trajectory and can be mistaken for sustained health improvement \cite{ma2021capacity}.

Transformer architectures provide a complementary solution by using self-attention to model dependencies over an entire sequence. Recent battery studies have used Transformer-based networks with compact feature extraction \cite{luo2023simple}, combined incremental-capacity analysis with attention-based temporal modeling \cite{xu2024incremental}, and evaluated Transformer estimators on real-world electric-vehicle data \cite{nakano2024advancing}. Attention has also been extended from individual cells to integral battery packs, where feature, temporal, and cell interactions must be modeled jointly \cite{hu2025packformer}. These results establish the usefulness of global interaction modeling for battery health assessment. Most such estimators, however, still learn their sequential representations from the target battery dataset alone, so the available aging samples bound the temporal knowledge acquired during training.

Large pretrained models offer a possible route beyond this limitation. Autoregressive language models such as GPT-2 and GPT-3 learn reusable sequence representations through large-scale pretraining \cite{radford2019language,brown2020language}. Their success has motivated foundation models designed for numerical time series. Chronos casts forecasting as token prediction \cite{ansari2024chronos}, MOMENT learns transferable representations for several time-series tasks \cite{goswami2024moment}, and Timer adapts generative pretraining to forecasting, imputation, and anomaly detection \cite{liu2024timer}. In the battery domain, BatteryGPT further demonstrates that generative pretraining can support early degradation prediction by autoregressively modeling operating features \cite{hu2026batterygpt}. Directly applying a pretrained backbone to SOH estimation remains nontrivial. Continuous HIs do not possess the discrete semantics of words; different indicators have distinct scales and noise characteristics; and long-term fading, transient recovery, and cross-indicator coupling must be represented simultaneously.

To address these challenges, we propose a \textbf{Multi-Health-Indicator-enhanced Pre-Trained Large Language Model (MHILLM)} for battery SOH estimation. MHILLM first constructs a set of scalar HIs from the available charging and discharging measurements of each record. Each HI sequence is then decomposed into a slowly varying trend and a short-term fluctuation component, enabling the model to distinguish persistent degradation from local recovery. A cross-HI attention module explicitly learns interactions among indicators, while a context token summarizes degradation statistics at the window level. The resulting numerical tokens are processed by a GPT-2 backbone adapted with low-rank updates \cite{hu2022lora}. Training proceeds in two stages: an autoregressive alignment objective first predicts the subsequent HI vector using training records only, and a lightweight regression head then maps the aligned representation to one-step-ahead SOH. The trainable tokenizer, cross-HI module, context representation, positional embeddings, normalization layers, regression head, and LoRA parameters are optimized, while the original attention and feed-forward projections remain frozen.

The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows.
\begin{itemize}
\item We develop a per-HI decomposition architecture that separately represents the long-term degradation trend and short-term fluctuations. This design preserves gradual capacity fade while reducing the ambiguity introduced by transient capacity regeneration.

\item We introduce cross-HI attention and a window-level context token to model complementary degradation evidence. The former captures adaptive interactions among heterogeneous indicators, whereas the latter supplies a compact summary of the current degradation stage.

\item We design a two-stage adaptation strategy for battery sequences. Autoregressive HI alignment transfers the sequential prior of the pretrained backbone to the battery domain, and parameter-efficient LoRA updates retain most pretrained weights while limiting the number of trainable backbone parameters.

\item We evaluate MHILLM on three public battery datasets under cell-disjoint protocols. The results demonstrate improved one-step-ahead SOH estimation on unseen test cells and are supported by component ablations, backbone comparisons, and parameter-sensitivity analyses.
\end{itemize}
